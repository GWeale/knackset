# Bryce Canyon’s big drop begins with the shuttle counter

Bryce Canyon’s published recreation visits fell from 2,498,075 in 2024 to 1,967,367 in 2025. That is a 21.2% decline, so the newsletter’s “one visitor in five” has an understandable numerical origin. But it gives readers the wrong impression: that a comparable count shows a fifth of the park’s visitors disappearing. The strongest explanation in the supplied evidence is a change in counting, with a much smaller decline remaining under the former method.

The revealing detail is that the park supplies two different totals for 2025. In its March 2026 [visitation release](https://home.nps.gov/brca/learn/news/2026-03-17-visitation-stats.htm), Bryce says earlier calculations used total shuttle boardings. Someone boarding repeatedly during a visit could therefore be counted repeatedly. Beginning with 2025 data, the park counted unique passengers entering via shuttle. That refinement changes how shuttle use contributes to the recreation-visit estimate. Comparing the new figure directly with the old one mixes a change in attendance with a change in measurement.

The park also estimates what 2025 would have looked like under its former total-boardings method: 2,437,461 recreation visits. Against 2024’s 2,498,075, that is a decrease of 60,614 visits. The calculation that matters is **60,614 ÷ 2,498,075 × 100 = 2.43%**, rounded to 2.4%. That is the useful comparison available here for readers trying to understand the change on a consistent counting basis.

The arithmetic also shows the scale of the measurement issue. The two 2025 figures differ by 470,094 visits. That difference accounts for about 89% of the 530,708-visit gap between the published annual totals. This is an arithmetic breakdown based on the park’s own reconstruction, not an independent audit of its counters or proof about exactly how many individual people came. Still, it strongly supports making the counting change the central explanation for the headline decline.

“Visitor” needs care for another reason. The [NPS definition](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/socialscience/visitor-use-statistics.htm) describes a visit as entry onto NPS lands or waters, with calculations governed by park-specific procedures. Recreation visits are not a census of distinct people over an entire year. Nor does counting unique passengers entering via shuttle mean that the resulting park-wide annual total represents unique individuals. We should describe these figures as recreation visits and avoid turning a percentage change in visits into a claim about people.

Could the 43-day partial government shutdown explain the remaining decline? It is a plausible contributor, but the supplied evidence cannot assign it a share. The park mentions the shutdown and says parks were kept open and accessible whenever possible. That does not establish unchanged access throughout the period, and it does not measure trips forgone. Without monthly comparable counts, operating details or a useful comparison, attributing the 2.4% decrease to the shutdown would turn a possible cause into an unsupported conclusion. Other changes in travel demand remain possible too.

The other supplied numbers do not settle that question. National recreation visits rose about 2% from 2023 to 2024, according to [NPS national totals](https://www.nps.gov/aboutus/visitation-numbers.htm), but that is a different year-to-year comparison from Bryce’s 2024–2025 change. It cannot establish whether Bryce diverged from a national trend in 2025. The park’s 11.6 million visitor hours and 153,741 overnight stays describe activity in 2025; without matching earlier figures, they cannot independently demonstrate a rise or fall.

Tell readers this: **Bryce Canyon reported 1.97 million recreation visits in 2025, but most of the 21% decline in its published total reflects revised shuttle counting. The park estimates that visits fell 2.4% under its former method. The available evidence does not isolate why that smaller decline occurred.**

This account uses only the supplied NPS transcriptions and data, not live retrieval. The most useful next evidence would be a consistently counted monthly series spanning both years, together with shutdown access records. That could change our understanding of the remaining attendance change; it would also test the park’s reconstruction that currently makes the dramatic headline misleading.
