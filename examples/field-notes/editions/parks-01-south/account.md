# Bryce Canyon’s big drop is mostly a change in the count

Bryce Canyon reported about one-fifth fewer recreation visits in 2025. But saying it “lost one visitor in five” turns a change in the recorded total into a claim about people staying away. The supplied National Park Service evidence supports a more careful account: most of the headline gap comes from a revised shuttle-counting method, while the park’s reconstruction under its former method shows a much smaller decline.

The published totals are striking: 2,498,075 recreation visits in 2024 and 1,967,367 in 2025. Subtracting the latter from the former produces 530,708 fewer recorded visits, a 21.2% fall. That arithmetic is correct. The problem is that the two totals were assembled differently. In its March 17, 2026 [visitation release](https://home.nps.gov/brca/learn/news/2026-03-17-visitation-stats.htm), Bryce Canyon explains that earlier counts used total shuttle boardings, potentially counting someone repeatedly within a visit. Beginning with 2025 data, it counted unique passengers entering via shuttle.

The revealing figure is the park’s alternative 2025 total. Had it continued using total boardings, the release says it would have reported 2,437,461 recreation visits. Compare that with the earlier-method 2024 figure and the decline becomes 60,614 visits, or 2.4%. The new method lowers the park’s reconstructed 2025 total by another 470,094 visits. That difference accounts for about 88.6% of the apparent year-to-year gap.

Here is the calculation that matters. Both year-to-year percentages use the same 2024 baseline; the final percentage describes the composition of the gap:

- Published comparison: (2,498,075 − 1,967,367) ÷ 2,498,075 × 100 = **21.2% lower**.
- Former-method comparison: (2,498,075 − 2,437,461) ÷ 2,498,075 × 100 = **2.4% lower**.
- Decomposition: **530,708 = 60,614 + 470,094 recreation visits**; 470,094 ÷ 530,708 × 100 = **88.6%**.

This is an accounting explanation, not a direct measurement of how many individuals changed their travel plans. The [NPS statistics guidance](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/socialscience/visitor-use-statistics.htm) defines a visit as entry onto agency-administered lands or waters and explains that park-specific procedures govern the calculation. A recreation visit is therefore not a distinct annual visitor. Nor does “unique passengers” in the shuttle procedure establish a count of unique people across the entire park and year. The newsletter should describe visits, and should identify the break in the series.

There could still have been a real downturn. Even the former-method reconstruction is below 2024. But that reconstruction is the park’s stated counterfactual, not a separate, independently audited count supplied here. It offers the best available comparison within this source pack; it does not establish that precisely 2.4% fewer people came, or isolate changes in shuttle use from changes in other visitor behavior.

The release also mentions a 43-day partial government shutdown and says parks were kept open and accessible whenever possible. That makes disruption a plausible contributor worth investigating. It does not measure a shutdown effect at Bryce Canyon. The supplied material gives no monthly comparison, specific access history or estimate of visits forgone. Assigning the residual decline to the shutdown would turn a possible explanation into an unsupported single-cause story.

Other supplied numbers cannot settle that question. Bryce logged 11,606,250 visitor hours and 153,741 overnight stays, but no comparable 2024 values are provided. National recreation visits rose between 2023 and 2024, according to the [NPS national totals](https://www.nps.gov/aboutus/visitation-numbers.htm); that earlier interval cannot show whether Bryce diverged from a national trend in 2025.

Tell readers that Bryce Canyon’s reported recreation visits fell 21.2%, largely because it changed how shuttle arrivals were counted; using its former method, the park estimates a 2.4% decline. The useful next question is what consistently counted monthly entries show alongside documented access changes. Those records could explain the smaller decline—or challenge the reconstruction on which this comparison depends. The supplied evidence supports a measurement-led explanation of the headline, while leaving the causes of any underlying downturn unresolved.

*Based only on the supplied NPS source transcription and counts, retrieved November 2, 2026; no live retrieval or independent audit was performed.*
