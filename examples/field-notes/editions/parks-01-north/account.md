# Bryce Canyon’s reported drop mostly reflects a counting change

Bryce Canyon recorded 1,967,367 recreation visits in 2025, a 21.2% decline from its reported 2024 total. That headline needs an immediate explanation: the park changed how it counted shuttle passengers. Its own estimate puts the decline at about 2.4% if the former method had continued. Saying Bryce Canyon “lost one visitor in five” turns a change in reported visits into an unsupported claim about how many people stopped coming.

The distinction comes from the park’s [March 17, 2026, visitation release](https://home.nps.gov/brca/learn/news/2026-03-17-visitation-stats.htm). Earlier counts used total shuttle boardings, which could count someone repeatedly within a visit. Beginning with the 2025 data, the park counted unique passengers entering via shuttle. The refinement therefore reduced the contribution of repeat boardings to the annual total. It does not mean the new annual figure is a count of distinct individuals who visited Bryce Canyon during the entire year.

The crucial comparison uses the park’s stated former-method estimate for 2025: 2,437,461 recreation visits. Set against the 2,498,075 reported for 2024, that is a decrease of 60,614 visits, or 2.43%. Comparing the newly reported 2025 figure directly with 2024 instead produces a decrease of 530,708 visits, or 21.24%. Both calculations are arithmetically correct. They answer different questions because the latter comparison crosses a change in measurement.

The size of that measurement difference is substantial. The two 2025 figures are 470,094 visits apart. That gap represents 88.6% of the apparent 530,708-visit decline between the published annual totals. This is an arithmetic breakdown using the park’s figures, rather than an independent audit of its counting system. It shows why the dramatic headline overstates the evidence for a collapse in attendance. Even the smaller decline remains a comparison of recreation visits as calculated under the former method, with all the limits that measure carries.

Those limits matter beyond this particular park. The National Park Service [defines a visit as entry onto NPS-administered lands or waters](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/socialscience/visitor-use-statistics.htm), and park-specific procedures govern the calculations. The agency says procedures can change with multipliers, detection technology, property or operational capacity. A recreation-visit total is therefore a defined statistical measure. Readers should not automatically interpret it as the number of unique people, nor assume that successive annual totals always use identical procedures.

What explains the smaller, comparable decline? The supplied evidence leaves that question open. Bryce Canyon’s release mentions a 43-day partial government shutdown and says parks remained open and accessible whenever possible. That establishes relevant context. It supplies no estimate of how many Bryce Canyon visits the shutdown affected, or evidence assigning the remaining decline to it. The duration alone cannot establish the shutdown’s effect. A single-cause account would require evidence connecting conditions at the park to changes in visits.

The other supplied figures offer context without resolving that question. Bryce Canyon reported 11,606,250 visitor hours and 153,741 overnight stays in 2025, but the source pack provides no comparable earlier values for either measure. National recreation visits increased about 2.0% from 2023 to 2024 in the NPS [national visitation figures](https://www.nps.gov/aboutus/visitation-numbers.htm). Those are different years from the Bryce Canyon comparison, so they cannot establish whether this park followed or diverged from a nationwide trend in 2025.

The accurate takeaway is clear: Bryce Canyon reported about 1.97 million recreation visits in 2025 after refining its shuttle count. Its published annual total fell 21.2%, with most of that apparent drop attributable to the difference between counting methods. On the park’s former-method estimate, visits declined about 2.4%. The supplied sources do not establish why that smaller decline occurred.
