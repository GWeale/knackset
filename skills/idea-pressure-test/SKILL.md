# Put an idea to a useful test

Turn an interesting hunch into a decision the user can act on: what must be true,
what could explain the evidence instead, and the smallest test worth doing next.
The result is a short pressure-test card, not a business plan or an interrogation.

## Start with the user's actual aim

Read the supplied idea, constraints, artifacts, and evidence before asking.
Establish what the user wants from it: enjoyment, learning, a useful tool, adoption,
income, or something else. A fun project need not prove willingness to pay.
Ask one missing question only when its answer could change the next test.
For a clear small request, do the requested work without imposing this process.

## Make the strongest fair case

Describe the idea's appeal in terms the user would recognize. State who benefits,
what changes for them, and the mechanism that might produce that change.
Distinguish a creative possibility from a claim about other people's behavior.
Identify the constraint that matters most: time, cost, access, quality, or reach.
Keep useful existing work and the option of a smaller version in view.

Choose the most consequential unresolved assumption. Write it as a claim that
could be wrong, with an observable consequence. Explain why its failure changes
the decision. Do not fill the card with every conceivable project risk.

## Look for evidence that could change your mind

Separate supplied observations, attributed reports, and your interpretations.
For material current claims, inspect relevant primary sources and date them when
tools and scope permit. Mark inaccessible evidence and the boundary of the search.
A quick search that finds nothing does not establish that no alternative exists.

Give the strongest contrary evidence, including evidence already in the user's
materials. Consider a serious rival explanation: apparent demand may be politeness,
or a prototype's appeal may come from personal facilitation rather than its tool.
Compare the idea with the current workaround, a smaller approach, or doing nothing
when that comparison helps. Do not invent objections just to balance the page.
If no contrary evidence is available, identify that gap and an observation that
would challenge the claim. An imagined failure is not an observed one.
Never invent interviews, customers, quotations, revenue, usage, or test results.

## Design the cheapest discriminating test

Choose a test whose possible results distinguish the important claim from its
rival. Prefer a manual sample, rough prototype, existing-data check, or small
observed task before a full build when it can answer the same question.
Match the test to the goal: a creative trial can test delight or feasibility;
an adoption claim needs behavior beyond praise, a waitlist, or stated interest.
Name the artifact, steps, participants or data needed, effort, cost, and measure.
Do not make unavailable people or services a prerequisite for useful preparation.

Before results are collected, state what would justify each outcome:

- **Continue:** the assumption survives well enough to justify the next investment.
- **Change:** a specific segment, mechanism, or smaller form merits a revised test.
- **Stop:** the evidence or cost rules out this version for the stated goal.
- **Inconclusive:** execution, measurement, access, or sample limits prevent a call.

Use thresholds supported by the user's constraints or baseline. Otherwise propose
transparent provisional criteria and explain the judgment behind them. A small
pilot informs this decision; it does not estimate a market or prove causation.
When results already exist, preserve the original criteria and report the outcome
against them. A revised threshold is a new decision, not a retroactive success.
Distinguish failure of the idea from a test that did not actually test it.

## Deliver the pressure-test card

Return a compact card containing:

1. **Idea and aim:** the bet, the intended benefit, and the relevant constraint.
2. **Strong case:** why it could work, with the evidence that supports it.
3. **Decisive unknown:** the consequential claim, contrary evidence, and best rival.
4. **Cheap test:** executable steps, resources, measure, and the four decision criteria.
5. **Decision now:** the recommended next action and what evidence would change it.

Link evidence beside the claim it supports. Keep unsupported statements labeled.
Research or a test plan does not authorize outreach, account creation, purchases,
publishing, or capturing private activity. Execute only work already authorized.
