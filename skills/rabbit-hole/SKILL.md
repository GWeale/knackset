# Follow a rabbit hole

Turn an interesting question into an explanation someone would want to read.
A useful investigation changes the model of the problem, rather than collecting
more links that repeat its opening premise.

## Find the question inside the question

Start with the puzzle, the audience, and what would count as a useful answer.
Read any supplied material before asking for context. If the request is broad,
choose a tractable thread and say why it is promising. Ask only when a missing
choice changes the investigation. A supplied-source task stays supplied-source;
use live public evidence when requested or needed and available.

Write down the appealing explanation and at least one plausible rival. These
are working hypotheses, not a requirement to manufacture an opposing side when
one claim is already settled. Look for the observation that would tell the
explanations apart. A trend may be a changed denominator, a new measurement,
a selection effect, or several causes acting together.

## Follow evidence, not repetition

Keep a small source trail: claim, original source, date/scope, and what it can
actually establish. Follow a quotation, chart or statistic to its origin when
that would change the conclusion. Ten articles repeating one press release
remain one evidential origin. Distinguish a correction from a new independent
observation, and a participant's account from an outside measurement.

Alternate discovery with a check that could make the current story fail:
- Reconstruct the timeline before assigning causes.
- Check the unit, denominator, baseline and who was counted.
- Look for a missing comparison or a plausible outside change.
- Seek the strongest evidence against the favored explanation.

Use only the moves the question needs. Do not turn a cultural history into a
pseudo-statistical study or pretend an unmeasured quantity can be calculated.
For numerical claims, carry the units through the calculation and show the
small calculation that matters. An inaccessible source remains a gap.

## Make the turn visible

Write the answer around the most consequential discovery. Open with the puzzle
or answer, show the few pieces of evidence that make it work, and explain what
the initial account missed. Use a timeline, comparison or causal sketch when it
makes the explanation clearer. Separate observed facts, a source's assertions,
and your interpretation at the point where the distinction matters.

Keep the interesting texture: a telling artifact, a surprising detail, or the
specific contradiction that sent the investigation in a new direction. Do not
invent a scene, quotation or discovery to make the narrative more satisfying.
Link the sources that support the actual claims. Mark supplied fictional
sources as fictional; do not dress them up as live retrieval.

Finish with the conclusion at its supported strength and the most useful open
question. Stop when the main rivals have been meaningfully tested, further
sources repeat existing evidence, or the agreed budget runs out. Explain which
remaining unknown could reverse the answer, rather than adding a generic caveat.

Deliver a readable account plus the compact evidence trail needed to inspect it.
The final shape can be an essay, field note, annotated timeline or investigation
brief. Match the user's intended medium; do not force a report template.
